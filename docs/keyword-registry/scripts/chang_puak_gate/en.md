I'm one of the white elephant statues standing at Chang Phueak Gate. To be precise, I'm one of a pair, standing side by side. Today let me tell you the story of this gate.

We were erected around 1400, when King Saen Mueang Ma had us built to honor two soldiers. Then in 1801 — the year right after Alessandro Volta invented the battery, if that helps you place it — King Kawila's brothers gave us new names. One of us became "the World Conqueror," the other "the Demon Conqueror" — grand titles indeed.

The moment I remember most vividly is March 9th, 1797. When King Kawila drove out the Burmese and returned to the city, he actually passed right in front of us and entered through this very gate. I watched that whole procession up close — circling the city, passing through this gate, heading on toward the royal palace.

There's also a legend tied to this area. Long ago, a white elephant carrying a sacred relic on its back is said to have set off from somewhere right around here, heading for Doi Suthep mountain. As a statue shaped like an elephant myself, that legendary living elephant's story doesn't feel like someone else's tale to me.

And even now, something fun keeps happening here. Since 2013, every year on April 11th and 12th, a procession called Yor Suai Wai Sa sets out from this gate and makes its way to the Three Kings Monument. The number of fingernail-dance participants grew from 150 in 2014 to 850 by 2019. I've stood in this same spot for centuries now, watching everything from a king's triumphant procession to today's young people dancing their way through.
