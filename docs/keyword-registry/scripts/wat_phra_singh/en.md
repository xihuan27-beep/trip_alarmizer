Hello. I'm Khruba Srivichai. I'm a monk, though people around here used to call me "the Tiger Monk." Let me tell you about the temple I spent the most time at in my whole life — Wat Phra Singh.

First, a fun fact. This place, now considered one of the most sacred temples in Chiang Mai — do you know what it used to be? Just a marketplace. Right up until a temple was built here in the mid-1300s.

The temple takes its name from its star attraction, the Phra Singh Buddha image. There's a legend about that statue. The story goes that in 1256, it was being shipped from Ceylon — modern-day Sri Lanka — when the ship wrecked. The statue supposedly washed ashore after drifting at sea for three days, landing at Nakhon Si Thammarat.

The historical record tells it a bit differently, though. The statue was actually in Kamphaeng Phet first, then a man named Thao Maha Phrom carried it to Chiang Rai, and his nephew, King Saen Mueang Ma, brought it to Chiang Mai sometime between 1385 and 1390. Around that same time, over in Europe, Geoffrey Chaucer had just started writing The Canterbury Tales — a fitting coincidence, since that's also a collection of legends and stories passed hand to hand. Think of the legend and the historical record as two different chapters of the same statue's story, not a contradiction.

Here's another fun bit — when the Phra Singh statue made its way to Chiang Rai, it actually traveled with a "sister statue." The Emerald Buddha, now considered the most sacred object in all of Thailand. For a while, that statue sat hidden inside a stucco covering in Chiang Rai, completely unknown to anyone, until it was finally discovered during King Tilok's reign. Today it sits in the royal palace in Bangkok, but once upon a time, it was hidden not far from here.

Wat Phra Singh is counted, alongside the Doi Suthep relic and the Inthakhin pillar, as one of Chiang Mai's three most sacred objects. That tells you just how special a place this temple held in the city's entire spiritual landscape.

Something else happened here in 1504. King Phaya Kaew had a massive Buddha statue cast, called Phra Chao Kao Tue, originally intended as the main image for this very temple. Why was it made? Back then, there was real tension between two rival sects — Wat Suan Dok and Wat Pa Daeng — and the statue was meant as a symbol of reconciliation between them. Except... it turned out too heavy to actually move here. A giant statue built to symbolize reconciliation, and it couldn't even make it to its own destination because of its weight. Kind of funny when you think about it, right? By the way, Lan Na temples line up their chedi and main hall along an east-west axis — that's not just architecture, it's a physical expression of Buddhist cosmology.

Alright, now for my part of the story. My connection to this temple wasn't an accident. There was a queen named Dara Rasami, famous for holding firmly to royal tradition her whole life. People often talk about her and me as complete opposites — her, the keeper of tradition; me, the rebel who stood with ordinary people. But here's the thing: she was the one who personally handpicked me to rebuild this exact temple. She entrusted other temples to other monks, but chose me specifically for this one. Isn't that something? Two people people talk about as opposite symbols, actually tangled up together like that.

So I became this temple's de facto abbot, and through the 1920s and '30s, I made it my base while leading a reform movement people called the "Tiger Order." This was the place where I really lived, taught, and made my home.

Then between 1935 and '36, things got genuinely hard. Nearly a thousand monks and novices who followed me were forcibly disrobed. It finally settled on April 21st, 1936, when a pledge was signed. "Reform" or "resistance" doesn't really capture it — this was something that upended a thousand people's lives.

Right in the middle of all that, on November 1st, 1935, I was arrested right here at this temple. When the authorities raided that day, a few of the young novices hid under the altar in the Lai Kham Hall. One of them remembered it later — crouched in that dark, cramped space under the altar, holding his breath, waiting for the footsteps to fade away.

I lived a few more years after that and passed away in 1938. Here's something interesting, though — if you visit the main hall today, you'll find a portrait of Dara Rasami hanging there, right alongside the male royals. People call me a symbol of resistance and her a guardian of tradition, but the two of us end up quietly sharing the same hall after all.

Oh, one more thing — after I died, my relics were divided into seven parts and enshrined in different places. One of them is at Wat Suan Dok. If you're planning to visit there too, you can hear more of my story there as well.
