I'm Ubon Wanna. I was born the youngest daughter of King Kawilorot of Chiang Mai. Today let me tell you about this residence, where I lived. My life had far less to do with royalty than it did with business — and with being a spirit medium.

First, let me tell you what kind of person I was. Here, I ran a textile workshop, a timber business, woodcarving, a lacquerware factory, and a distillery, all at once. I kept trading relationships with merchants from America, Britain, Scandinavia, and Burma, and I spoke English reasonably well. Once, I gave an English visitor a weaving demonstration right on this veranda — and took an order for English lace on the spot. I may have been born a princess, but I lived my life as an international businesswoman in my own right.

There's something else worth mentioning here. I was the one who personally discovered that Bunthawong, a former uparat — a viceroy — had died from an opium overdose. Later, though, I became my family's spirit medium, and in that role, I was the one who summoned his spirit and gave it voice. The woman who found his death became, in time, the one who spoke for him.

As medium, I channeled his spirit and recommended abolishing the liquor tax — the monopoly on arak. Remarkably, in 1884, the Siamese government actually abolished it. It was a rare case where a spirit ritual led to an actual change in policy.

Something else happened in 1881 — the same year Tsar Alexander II of Russia was assassinated, if that helps you place it in time. I got caught up in what came to be called the Mong Pong Ya case. The man I loved was a Burmese merchant who held British subject status, and he was found dead under suspicious circumstances. I ended up as one of ten defendants tried in an extraterritorial court. It was a case tangled up in business, love, and international law, all at once.

In 1884, my sister Thip Kesorn fell fatally ill. Around her illness, a power struggle broke out between the traditional spirit-medium faction and those who favored Western medicine. It was a chaotic time — spirit rituals and negotiations over tax resistance were unfolding at the same moment. Her death wasn't just a personal loss for me; it was also a political storm.

When my sister passed, I took in my young niece, Dara Rasami. She was only eleven at the time. But as it happened, that same year, I too passed away. Losing my sister, taking in my niece, and leaving this world myself — all within one turbulent year.

Here's the part I find interesting, though — the story doesn't end there. After I was gone, my own children, Kawiloweng and Lenu Wanna, were in turn taken in and raised by that same young niece, Dara Rasami, once she'd grown up. Just as I had cared for her, she grew up to care for my children. Across one generation, that care came right back around. My interest in weaving passed to her the same way.

Looking back, my life was a tangle of royal blood and a merchant's boldness, the mystery and scandal of being a spirit medium, and a deep sense of responsibility to family. It was short and intense, but its trace carried on — to the next generation, and the one after that.
