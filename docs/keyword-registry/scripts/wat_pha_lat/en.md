Hello. I'm Khruba Srivichai. I'm a monk, though people around here used to call me "the Tiger Monk." Today let me tell you about a smaller, quieter place: Wat Pha Lat.

Long ago, there was a pilgrimage route running from inside the Chiang Mai city walls all the way to the summit of Doi Suthep. This temple sits right in the middle of that route, at just about the perfect spot to rest. It's next to a waterfall, so tired pilgrims would have paused here to catch their breath.

In 1898 — around the time Marie Curie discovered radium and polonium, if that helps place it — a man named Prince Damrong passed through here. He was touring the provinces to prepare for a new administrative system called the monthon thesaphiban. Here's the interesting part, though: photographs taken during that visit are what let us know today how far back this temple's buildings go. A royal inspection photo turned out to be the clue to this old temple's age.

The temple's main building, Hor Phra Chao at Pha Lat Waterfall, is built of brick in a Burmese colonial style, with a five-arched façade. It's built right up against the natural cliff face, so the waterfall, the rock, and the building almost blend into one.

Why did Burmese-style architecture find its way into this small mountain temple? Around 1856, timber trade between Britain and Burma picked up, and workers from Burma, the Shan states, and Pa-O communities came here for that trade. Over time, some of them became religious patrons, and that influence stayed embedded in temples like this one.

The temple's original design followed a "five Buddhas of the era" concept — three Buddhas of the past, a Buddha of the present age (shown reclining, though that image was later lost), and a Buddha of the future — trying to hold past, present, and future all within one building.

Now let me tell you my part. This temple's main hall was built by Burmese craftsmen, during the same era as me. The roof was originally thatch, but it was later changed to tile — and, as it happens, right around the time I was building the road up to Doi Suthep. So that roof itself carries a trace of that period. You'll also find my mark here too, a tiger carved into the rock.

Around that same time, there was tension between the state and the monastic order over the building of that Doi Suthep road. Scholars still debate exactly why I was first arrested, and that whole backdrop overlaps with this very period, right around here.

Looking back, my story wasn't only made up of big, dramatic events. Building temples, laying roads, getting arrested, arguing with people — in between all of that, a small resting place like this one quietly held its ground. Maybe you'll take a moment here too, and catch your breath.
