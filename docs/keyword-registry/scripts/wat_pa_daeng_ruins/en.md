My name is Ratanapañña. Funnily enough, exactly where I came from — and even whether this was really my true name — became a matter of ongoing debate among later scholars. Today let me tell you about this temple, Wat Pa Daeng. It's my story too.

I belonged to the Sihalabhikkhu, the Sinhalese-lineage monastic order. It began in 1424 — around the time of the Battle of Verneuil in the Hundred Years' War in Europe, if that helps you place it — with the first generation of monks re-ordained in Sri Lanka, as a reform movement. We followed the aranyavasi tradition, living and practicing in the forest, and this temple was our home base. It later appears in the legend of the Buddha who walked the whole world, under the name "Asokaram."

Something happened in 1504. King Phaya Kaew had a massive Buddha image cast, called Phra Chao Kao Tue, meant as a symbol of reconciliation between two long-feuding sects — mine, Wat Pa Daeng, and Wat Suan Dok. I've heard the statue turned out too heavy to reach its intended destination, Wat Phra Singh, and settled at Wat Suan Dok instead — but either way, mine was one of the two sects that statue was meant to reconcile.

In 1511, a boundary-consecration ceremony was held at Wat Jet Yot. About twenty monks attended, and not a single one on that list was from Chiang Mai. I bring this up now because, much later, that very fact became a key clue in the debate over my own identity. I had no idea at the time that the simple fact of my absence from that list would give scholars centuries later such a headache.

Between 1516 and 1517 — right around the time Martin Luther was about to post his Ninety-Five Theses in Europe, if that helps you place it — I began writing a chronicle here at this temple called the Jinakalamali. I kept adding to it until 1527. That book would go on to become one of the most important sources for understanding the history of Lan Na. I was the one who recorded other people's history — the history of kings, the history of cities.

But here's the irony. No one ever properly recorded my own history in return. A scholar named Prachakit in 1907, Prince Damrong in 1908, and a scholar named Cœdès in 1915 — all three reached different conclusions. Exactly where I came from, and what my true name really was, remains unresolved to this day.

The man who so carefully recorded everyone else's history ended up leaving his own wrapped in fog. Maybe that's simply the fate of anyone who writes history down.
