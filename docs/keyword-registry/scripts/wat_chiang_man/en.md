# Wat Chiang Man (English)

Wat Chiang Man is the oldest temple in Chiang Mai — in many ways, the city's original "founding office."

In 1296, the ambitious King Mangrai allied with King Ngam Mueang of Phayao and King Ruang of Sukhothai to scout the site for a new capital. The temple's own founding inscription records that all three kings jointly established it. Following the old Lan Na belief that "a city must have a temple to anchor it," the temple was named Chiang Man — "the firm city." That same year, 1296, Marco Polo had just returned to Venice from his travels across China.

Fresh from conquering the kingdom of Hariphunchai, the king brought two precious Buddha images here: Phra Setangkhamani, carved from white jade, and a Gupta-style stone image depicting the Buddha subduing the elephant Nalagiri. The temple also appears as one of eight sacred sites in the legend of the "World-Touring Buddha" — a sign of just how central Wat Chiang Man was to Chiang Mai's sacred geography from the very start.

Some 170 years later, in 1471, King Tilokarat rebuilt the temple's collapsed original chedi as the "Chang Lom Chedi," its base ringed by fifteen elephant statues. (Across Europe, the Wars of the Roses were raging in England, with Edward IV recently restored to the throne.)

But the temple's fortunes soon turned. In 1551, Chiang Mai fell to Burmese forces, and the temple was abandoned. (In Europe, the Council of Trent was underway, reshaping Catholic Christianity in response to the Reformation.) Yet just seven years later, in 1558, the very king who now ruled over the conquered city — Burmese King Mangthra — became its lavish patron, funding its restoration. A rare case of a conqueror protecting the faith of the conquered. (That same year, Elizabeth I ascended the English throne.)

*(A scholar's footnote: an inscription dated 1581 claims the temple already had a "triple wall" as early as 1296 — a detail found in no other chronicle, leading historians to suspect it may be a 16th-century embellishment. In Europe that year, the Dutch provinces had just formally declared their independence from Spain.)*

Nearly five hundred years after the founding, on Thursday, 9 March 1797 — the exact 500th anniversary of Mangrai's city — King Kawila drove out the Burmese and made his triumphant return to a long-empty Chiang Mai. The night before entering the royal palace, he chose to spend it right here, in front of this temple. Kawila would go on to rebuild the depopulated city with his famous policy of "putting people into cities as vegetables into baskets," recalling scattered families to resettle the capital. (In Europe, Napoleon was just concluding his Italian campaign with the Treaty of Campo Formio.)

By the late 19th century, monks of the Dhammayutika sect took up residence here for the first time in Chiang Mai. (Across Europe, the Eiffel Tower had just opened in Paris.)

In 1909, Queen Dara Rasami removed one of the temple's Buddha images and took it to Bangkok. When her half-brother, Inthawarorot — then governor of Chiang Mai — died suddenly just two months later, locals whispered that it was an ill omen brought on by that very act. (That same year in Europe, Louis Blériot became the first person to fly across the English Channel.)

In the 1920s and '30s, the temple became the stage for another story entirely. Khruba Srivichai, a monk born in the Year of the Tiger, made it a base for what became known as the "Tiger Order" — a reform movement among his followers. Buildings he restored still bear a tiger motif, regarded to this day as his personal "signature." He was once investigated on a charge of possessing "a magic sword with a golden scabbard," an accusation with unmistakable overtones of rebellion. Exactly when and why he was first arrested remains disputed among historians — the long-standing account places it around 1908–10 for violating monastic law, while more recent research points instead to around 1915, for violating conscription law. (This was the height of Japan's colonial rule over Korea — and, across interwar Europe, a fragile peace was drifting toward another war.)

Twenty-four years after his death, in 1962, a memorial hall was built within the temple grounds in his honor. (That same year, the world held its breath through the Cuban Missile Crisis.)

Today, Wat Chiang Man holds two contrasting symbols side by side: Dara Rasami, guardian of royal tradition, and Khruba Srivichai, the rebel monk who stood with the people. For more than seven hundred years, this one small temple has carried the story of a city — its birth, its occupation and liberation, and the intertwined fates of its royals and its commoners.
