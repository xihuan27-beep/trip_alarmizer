Hey there. I'm Khruba Srivichai — a monk, though people around here used to call me "the Tiger Monk." I want to tell you the story of this temple, Wat Chiang Man, the place I spent my whole life. Everything that happened before I was even born, I only know because the old monks here told me. And what happened after I died... well, I'll get to that. The story's not over yet.

Let's start from the very beginning. It's the year 1296. A young, ambitious king named Mangrai was hunting for the perfect spot to build a new capital. He didn't pick it alone — he brought in two other kings, Ngam Mueang of Phayao and Ruang of Sukhothai, to help him scout the land. Kind of like calling up your two savviest friends before signing a lease. Together, the three kings founded this very temple. You can still see all three of their names carved into the old foundation stone today.

Why start with a temple, before the city even existed? There's an old Lan Na belief that a city needs a temple to hold it together. So they named it Chiang Man — "the solid city." They wanted the city to stand firm before they'd even laid the first brick. If you need a time marker: over in Europe, Marco Polo had just made it back to Venice after years traveling through China.

Fresh off conquering the kingdom of Hariphunchai, the king brought two precious Buddha images here — Phra Setangkhamani, carved from white jade, and a stone carving in the Indian Gupta style, showing the Buddha taming a wild elephant. Bringing the most sacred treasures from a conquered land to your brand-new capital's first temple wasn't just showing off. It was basically saying: this land's spiritual power belongs to us now, too. This temple even shows up in the legend of the Buddha who walked the whole world, as one of eight sacred stops on his journey. So from day one, Wat Chiang Man wasn't just another temple — it was somewhere special.

Of course, it wasn't smooth sailing forever. About 170 years later, in 1471, King Tilokarat rebuilt the temple's crumbling stupa — the "Chang Lom Chedi," fifteen elephant statues wrapped around its base. You can still see it standing today.

But the good times didn't last. In 1551, Chiang Mai fell to Burmese forces, and the temple was abandoned. When the temple of a conquered city gets abandoned, that's not just an empty building sitting there — it's a sign the whole city lost its ability to protect itself.

Here's the twist, though. Just seven years later, in 1558, the very man now ruling the conquered city — the Burmese king Mangthra — became the temple's biggest supporter and paid to rebuild it. Why would a conqueror do that? Probably because holding a place takes more than a sword. You need people's hearts too.

Quick note here, from the historians: there's an inscription from 1581 claiming the temple already had a "triple wall" back in 1296. Problem is, that detail shows up nowhere else — no other record mentions it. So scholars suspect somebody tacked that bit on later, as the legend grew with time. Old temples tend to pick up stories like that.

Back to the main thread. Almost 500 years after the founding, on Thursday, March 9th, 1797 — exactly the 500th anniversary of Mangrai founding the city — King Kawila drove out the Burmese and made his triumphant return to a Chiang Mai that had sat empty for years. The night before he entered the royal palace, he chose to spend it right here, in front of this temple. Probably not a coincidence — one last night at the oldest, holiest temple before starting something new. Afterward, Kawila brought the scattered people back and rebuilt the city with a policy he liked to sum up as "packing people into cities the way you pack vegetables into baskets."

Toward the end of the 1800s, monks from a new sect called Dhammayutika settled here for the very first time in Chiang Mai — a sign the city was slowly coming under the reach of the royal court in Bangkok.

One more story before I get to my own part. When I first arrived at this temple, the older monks were still whispering about something from 1909. Queen Dara Rasami had taken one of the temple's Buddha images with her to Bangkok. Two months later, her half-brother Inthawarorot, who was governor of Chiang Mai at the time, died suddenly. A monk's diary from that exact period actually survives — and it says, word for word, "a very inauspicious event." People believed his death was karma for moving that statue. Even by the time I arrived, folks were still talking about it.

Now for my part of the story. I was born in the Year of the Tiger — which might explain why every building I restored ended up with a tiger carved somewhere on it. You can still spot them on the gables today. People started calling it my "signature."

In the 1920s and '30s, I made this temple my base while leading a reform movement. People later called it the "Tiger Order." Honestly, I was just fixing up buildings and teaching people — but the authorities kept eyeing me with suspicion. At one point, they investigated me for supposedly owning "a magic sword with a golden sheath." Sounds ridiculous now, doesn't it? But back then, that kind of accusation practically screamed rebellion.

I really was arrested at one point, and here's the funny part — historians still argue about exactly why. Different official records give different reasons. Some say I broke monastic law, others say I dodged military conscription. Around that same time, over in Europe, the First World War had only just ended, and the continent was still picking up the pieces.

Alright, here's the last part of my story. I passed away in 1938. But the story didn't end there.

Twenty-four years later, in 1962, they built a shrine here at the temple in my honor. And then in 2011, of all places, a politician chose that very shrine to announce a run for prime minister — holding me up as a symbol of standing with ordinary people. I never saw that coming while I was alive.

So today, Wat Chiang Man holds this strange, wonderful pairing — Dara Rasami on one side, guardian of royal tradition, and me on the other, the rebel monk who stood with the people. For over 700 years, this one small temple has carried the entire story of a city: its birth, its occupation, its liberation, and the tangled lives of royals and everyday people alike. Next time you're here, look up at the gables for a tiger carving. That's me.

Oh, one more thing — if you're planning to visit Wat Phra Singh too, that's actually where I spent most of my time. People pretty much treated me as the real abbot there. Something big happened there in 1935, too. If you're curious, come find me — I'll pick the story back up when you get there.
