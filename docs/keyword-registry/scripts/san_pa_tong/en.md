Hello. I'm Khruba Srivichai. I'm a monk, though people around here used to call me "the Tiger Monk." Today let me tell you about San Pa Tong.

This region was always a place where many different people came together to live. In 1797, after King Kawila drove out the Burmese and returned, he carried out a policy he liked to describe as "putting people into cities the way you pack vegetables into baskets." Under that policy, five different peoples — the Tai Yuan, Tai Khün, Tai Lü, Tai Yong, Lawa, and Mon — all settled together right here in San Pa Tong. That kind of gathering of so many different roots in one place wasn't common.

But this land's history goes back much further than that. At a place called Wiang Tha Kan, there once stood an outpost city from the Hariphunchai era, roughly 1,200 years ago. Human bones, horse bones, and pottery have been excavated from that very site. People were living on this land long before King Kawila's time.

There's an interesting legend told here too. Long ago, a giant eel appeared in a village, and the villagers agreed to share it among themselves. But they left out one poor widow entirely. As punishment, the whole village sank into the ground and became what's now Nong Sarian Lake. It's a story about never neglecting the poor — a very different kind of tale from the grand royal histories.

Now let me tell you my part. In 1927 — not long after the First World War had ended, if that helps place the era — I restored a temple here in San Pa Tong called Wat Phra Bat Yang Wat, which had fallen into ruin. Like many other temples, this was one I put my own hands to.

But in 1936, something truly difficult happened. The abbots of three temples in this region were forced to disrobe. One of them was Panya, the abbot of that very temple I had restored, Wat Phra Bat Yang Wat. Records show that of the entire Monthon Phayap region, San Pa Tong stood out as the place that resisted most fiercely. Other areas mostly went along quietly, but the people here held out to the very end.

Looking back, San Pa Tong was a land where many different roots took hold together from the very beginning — and maybe that's exactly why, when the crisis came, this was the place that held together the most fiercely of all.
