I'm Dara Rasami. I was born a princess of Chiang Mai, and later became a queen consort of Siam. Today I want to tell you about this house, Dara Phirom Palace, where I spent my final years — looking back on my whole life as I go.

I was sent away from Chiang Mai to the royal court in Bangkok when I was very young. From the outside, it might have looked like a glamorous life — I became a consort of the King of Siam, after all. But what that life actually felt like from the inside was quite different from how it looked.

In 1889, something happened that came to be called the "secret letter" incident. My father had sent a letter discussing an alliance with Chiang Tung, and I passed it along to the king exactly as it was. What came back was a threatening reply. That was when I understood something clearly — I might have been a queen in name, but in practice I was closer to a political hostage binding Chiang Mai to Bangkok.

Life inside the palace wasn't easy either. I faced discrimination, sometimes subtle and sometimes not, simply for being "Lao." While people called me an exotic, mysterious queen from the north, this was the reality underneath that image.

The hardest thing of all happened in 1892. My only daughter died at just two and a half years old. The king would later blame himself, saying it had been "his mistake." Beneath all the politics and titles, I was, in the end, simply a mother who had lost her child. That was around the time the Eiffel Tower had just gone up in Paris, if that helps you place it.

Nearly twenty years passed, and on February 12th, 1909, I finally returned home to Chiang Mai. Before I left, following northern tradition, I washed the king's feet with my own hair as a farewell. What's strange is that in that very moment of parting, I was also elevated to a higher title, "Phra Rachaya." A moment of leaving and a moment of promotion, at once — political loyalty and personal affection tangled together in a very complicated feeling. If you need a time marker: this was around when Japan's colonial rule over Korea was taking hold.

Even after returning to Chiang Mai, things weren't entirely smooth. I once had a Buddha image moved from a temple, and when my brother died suddenly not long after, people whispered that it had been an ill omen. Whatever my intentions were, my actions always seemed to get read differently by others.

In 1914, I laid my father's remains to rest at the summit of a mountain called Doi Angkhan. And I renamed that mountain "Doi Inthanon." It was the last tribute I could offer him.

Then, from around 1915 to 1920, I acquired seventy rai of land near a village called Mae Rim and built this house. I called it "Suan Chao Sabai" — a garden of ease and comfort. Having stepped away entirely from the political stage, this was a life I built for myself.

I did a lot of new things here. I learned how to grow cabbage from a man named Sithiphon Kridakon, and grew melons good enough to sell to hotels in Bangkok. I introduced a new variety of lamyai here too. People started calling me a "matron" of modern agriculture.

I did a great deal of cultural work as well. I formed a Lan Na dance troupe called Rong Kii, and created dances here — the fingernail dance and the candle dance among them. A good deal of what people now think of as Lan Na culture actually began right in this house. I also passed down weaving traditions, an interest I'd inherited from my aunt Ubon Wanna, and this house became the base for medical patronage too — expanding McCormick Hospital and supporting the McKean leper colony.

Around that time, there was a monk whose name kept coming up as a kind of opposite symbol to mine. People called me the one who preserved royal tradition, and him the rebel who stood with ordinary people. What's funny is that I was well aware, even then, that I too was being consumed as a "symbol" in just the same way. People always seem to want to trap someone inside a single image.

I died on December 9th, 1933. There's one thing I'd like to set straight — many visitors come here assuming my remains are here. Actually, they're divided in half, resting at Wat Suan Dok and at Wat Ratchabophit in Bangkok. This house was never my tomb. It was simply where I lived.

After I was gone, this house had its own winding history. The military used it through the 1940s to the 1960s, and afterward, Chulalongkorn University took on its restoration. That's how, in 1990, it opened as the museum you're standing in right now.

Looking back, my life was a tangle of glamour and loss, honor and discrimination, politics and love, all at once. This house was the place I finally chose for myself, after living through all of it.

Oh, one more thing — that mountain I renamed for my father, Doi Inthanon, still carries my mark too. If you're planning to visit there as well, you can hear more of my story there.
