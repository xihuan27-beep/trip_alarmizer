I'm a monk who has looked after this temple, Wat Chedi Luang, for a very long time. Honestly, I couldn't tell you exactly since when — that's how old this story is. Today let me tell you about this temple.

You know it today as "Wat Chedi Luang," but it carried several other names before that. Wat Chotikaram, Wat Ratchaphotthikan, Wat Ratchakutaram — people called it something different in each era. Under the name "Chotikaram," it even appears in the legend of the Buddha who walked the whole world, as one of the eight sacred temples.

Like other Lan Na temples, this one places its chedi at the center, with the main hall lined up facing east on the same axis. It's not just architecture — it's Buddhist cosmology centered on Mount Meru, along with astrology built around nine planets, laid out in stone.

But the most important event in this temple's history happened in 1545 — around the time the Council of Trent had just opened in Europe, if that helps you place it. That year, a great earthquake struck, and the spire of this temple's massive chedi — once one of the tallest structures in all of Lan Na — came crashing down. It's never been fully restored since. That "unfinished" silhouette you see today is actually the mark left by that disaster, all those centuries ago.

But here's the interesting part — this temple, with its half-ruined tower, ended up becoming the spiritual center of the city instead. In 1800, King Kawila moved the city pillar, called the Inthakhin, here from Wat Sadue Mueang. From that day on, this imperfect temple ended up holding the most central place in the whole city. Even today, this Inthakhin pillar is counted, alongside the Doi Suthep relic and the Phra Singh Buddha image, among Chiang Mai's three most sacred objects.

Another change came in the 1960s. When City Hall took formal charge of the Inthakhin ceremony, the spirit-medium elements that had originally been part of it were split off and moved to a place called Jaeng Sri Phum. What had once been a single rite split, over time, into two branches — an official Buddhist ceremony on one side, and folk spirit-medium belief on the other.

Looking back, this temple is a genuinely ironic place. Its tallest tower collapsed and was never made whole again — and yet, it was precisely after that imperfection that it earned the most sacred spot in the entire city. Not perfection, but the wound itself, is what made it the center.

Oh, one more thing — the spirit-medium tradition that split off from the Inthakhin ceremony in the 1960s still continues today, at a place called Jaeng Sri Phum. If you're planning to visit there too, you can hear more there as well.
