My name hasn't survived. I was a rice merchant at Sip Market in Wiang Kum Kam. Today let me tell you the story of this city, Wiang Kum Kam.

In 1286 — around the same time, over in the Netherlands, one of the worst floods in recorded history, the St. Lucia's Flood, struck — King Mangrai founded this city. It was a solid fortress, ringed on every side by moats and stockades. Its name was "Kum Kam," which in the northern dialect means "the fortress that controls water." Looking back now, that name itself feels like a chilling warning of what was to come.

But just five years after its founding, in 1296, King Mangrai left this place behind and founded a new capital, Chiang Mai. It was a remarkably short life as a capital city. Still, people like me kept living here afterward. This was still our home.

Around 1411, we built a great dyke with our own hands, trying to hold back the river that kept overflowing into the city. For a long time, people assumed it was just a natural mound that had formed on its own — but in truth, we built it ourselves, by hand. I remember lending my own effort to that work. Fighting the water was simply part of our lives.

But over the following decades, the Ping River kept shifting its course, little by little, edging closer and closer to our city. It didn't happen overnight. It felt more like a hand slowly closing around us.

Then, in 1524 — the same year the German Peasants' War broke out in Europe, if that helps you place it — a great flood finally came. At Sip Market, the very market where I sold rice, people drowned. They were my neighbors. I still haven't forgotten that day.

A city named "the fortress that controls water" ended up sinking beneath that very water. For a very long time afterward, this city lay completely buried in mud. It slowly faded from people's memory too.

It was only much later that people began digging up this ground again, and that's how these ruins you're walking through now came back into the world. Somewhere right where you're standing might just be the very spot where I once sold rice.
