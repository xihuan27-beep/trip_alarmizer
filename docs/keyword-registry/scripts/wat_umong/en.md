I'm Thera Jan. I was a scholar-monk. Today let me tell you about this temple, Wat Umong. But first, I should tell you something — a lot of this story is uncertain. Not just to me, but to later scholars too. I'll tell you that uncertainty honestly, right along with everything else.

First, let me explain how I ended up this way. I received wisdom from a goddess at Doi Suthep. But apparently, once I had that wisdom, I wasn't humble about it. As punishment, I was cursed — told that I would go mad someday. So the story I'm telling you now is, in a way, the story of someone who kept learning and teaching even while sensing the madness that was coming for him.

In the mid-1380s, King Kuna invited me here. At the time, this ground had eleven clumps of bamboo growing on it, so it was called "Welukattharam" — the Bamboo Monastery. But honestly, I can't be certain that the temple you're standing in right now is truly the same Welukattharam from back then. All that survives is circumstantial evidence.

After that, I served as an advisor to two kings in succession — first Kuna, then Tilokarat. For King Tilokarat, I even wrote something called a "guide to conduct."

And in the year I turned seventy-seven, around 1445 — the same year Gutenberg was developing movable-type printing in Europe, if that helps you place it — I passed away. But the place where I died is recorded separately, as "Wat Umong Noi." Whether that's the same place as this temple or somewhere else entirely, I honestly can't say for sure.

Here's another interesting thing. There's a legend that King Mangrai founded this temple around the year 1300. But when you actually look at the murals and the shape of the chedi, they seem to date from somewhere between 1380 and the 1550s. As a scholar myself, I have to say — when legend and evidence disagree like that, it's genuinely interesting.

Now, one last confession. Everything I've just told you rests on a single document. It's called the Banha Thera Jan manuscript, and the original is long lost — only later copies survive. It isn't arranged in proper chronological order, and it has its share of errors. So this whole story you just heard is, in truth, hanging by the thread of one thin, imperfect copy.

Even after I was gone, plenty happened to this temple. Around 1900, it was looted. Then, starting in 1948, a man named Chao Chuen Sirorot began restoring it. But during the restoration, while clearing away sand, the lower portions of the murals were lost all over again. Something else slipped away, even in the middle of the work meant to save it.

Someone who gained wisdom but wasn't humble enough, and was cursed with madness for it — and whose entire record has since faded down to one incomplete copy. Maybe that's the ending that suits this temple best, after all.
